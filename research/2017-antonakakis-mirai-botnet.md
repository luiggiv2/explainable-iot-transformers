- **Dataset(s)**: Own multi-vantage-point measurement corpus: a network telescope/darknet, active Telnet honeypots, passive and active DNS (Censys), and captured C2 attack-command logs, spanning a 7-month period (roughly July 2016–February 2017) covering Mirai's rise to a peak of ~600,000 infections.
- **Models / Methods**: Empirical measurement study (not a classifier) — malware-binary phylogeny/clustering, scan-fingerprint-based infection tracking, and C2 attack-command log analysis.
- **Key metrics reported**: Mirai's scanning phase sends asynchronous, stateless TCP SYN probes to pseudorandom IPv4 addresses on Telnet ports **TCP/23 and TCP/2323**; on a hit, it brute-forces a Telnet login using 10 randomly chosen username/password pairs drawn from a **hardcoded dictionary of 62 credentials**. Infections peaked at **~600,000 devices** (late Nov 2016), held a steady state of 200,000–300,000 (Sept 2016), and collapsed to ~100,000 after the source code was publicly released. Between Sept 27, 2016 and Feb 28, 2017, the botnet issued **15,194 distinct DDoS attack commands** against 5,046+ distinct targets, roughly evenly split across application-layer, volumetric, and TCP-state-exhaustion classes: HTTP flood (2,736 attacks/1,035 targets), UDP-PLAIN flood (2,542/1,278), UDP flood (2,440/1,479), ACK flood (2,173/875), SYN flood (1,935/764), GRE-IP flood (994/587), ACK-STOMP flood (830/359), VSE flood (809/550, targets Valve Source Engine game servers), DNS flood ("water torture," 417/173, targets open resolvers), GRE-ETH flood (318/210). About half of scanning bots sent fewer than 10,000 scan packets total, and the majority scanned at an estimated rate below 250 bytes/second — several thousand times slower than non-IoT worms like SQL Slammer (1.5 MB/s) or Witty (3 MB/s), consistent with resource-constrained embedded hardware.
- **XAI method (if any)**: None (measurement/forensic study, not a trained classifier).
- **Relevance to our RQs**: Primary ground-truth source for RQ2 on both the Mirai and DDoS attack categories — establishes destination ports 23/2323 and the TCP SYN flag as the canonical scanning signature, and gives a concrete taxonomy of flood attack types with their typical target ports/protocols (e.g., DNS flood on port 53, HTTP flood on port 80). Directly informs RQ3: since CICIoT2023's Mirai traffic is generated from "adapted Mirai source code," this paper lets us judge whether model attributions track genuine Mirai behavior (port 23/2323 dominance, SYN-only scanning, credential-guessing pattern) or testbed-specific artifacts.
- **Stated limitations**: Measurements are vantage-point-dependent (darknet, honeypot, and passive-DNS coverage can undercount infections or misattribute botnet variants); the scan-rate/bandwidth estimate is explicitly called a "strict underestimate" because bots interleave scanning with C2 command processing and brute-force attempts; attribution of some high-profile outages (e.g., Liberia/Lonestar Cell) to Mirai's network effect could not be fully established and is noted as contested by other researchers.
- **Quotable finding (paraphrased)**: Mirai's entire footprint rested on a strikingly simple recipe — stateless SYN scanning concentrated on two Telnet ports plus a 62-credential brute-force dictionary — yet this was enough for a botnet of low-bandwidth embedded devices to briefly control over 600,000 hosts and launch more than 15,000 DDoS attacks spanning ten distinct flood techniques in five months.

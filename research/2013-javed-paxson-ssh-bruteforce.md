# Detecting Stealthy, Distributed SSH Brute-Forcing
- **Authors**: Mobin Javed, Vern Paxson
- **Year / Venue**: 2013, Proceedings of the 20th ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security (ACM CCS 2013), pp. 85–96. Top-tier security venue (CORE A*, Q1-equivalent).
- **DOI**: 10.1145/2508859.2516719
- **URL**: https://www.icir.org/vern/papers/dist-ssh-det.ccs13.pdf (also https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2508859.2516719)
- **Cluster**: 4
- **Dataset(s)**: 8 years of centrally-syslogged SSH authentication records from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (~4,000 employees/visitors), cross-correlated with external sources (other monitored sites, blocklists) for validation.
- **Models / Methods**: Change-point detection on the aggregate SSH login-failure-rate time series to bracket candidate attack epochs in time; a beta-binomial model of legitimate users' authentication-error behavior used to parameterize detection thresholds trading off false positives vs. time-to-detection; a "coordination glue" host-clustering step to separate coordinated distributed brute-forcers from independent legitimate failures.
- **Key metrics reported**: Found dozens of distributed, stealthy brute-force campaigns across the 8-year dataset; the longest coordinated campaign spanned **~1.5 months**; attacking hosts showed an average **~70% overlap** with hosts simultaneously attacking other independent sites (evidence of shared botnet infrastructure); roughly half of the large-scale incidents observed at the site were part of coordinated/global campaigns rather than isolated local attacks. The paper's related-work discussion (Vykopal et al.; Hellemons et al.) further documents that SSH brute-force flows typically appear as many short flows with small, similar byte counts in both directions, clustered tightly in time, and can be modeled as a three-phase process — scanning, brute-force, die-off — trackable via flows-per-second, packets-per-flow, and bytes-per-packet.
- **XAI method (if any)**: None.
- **Relevance to our RQs**: Ground truth for RQ2 on the brute-force attack category: the discriminative signal is aggregate/temporal (many short, near-uniform flows clustered in time; elevated login-failure rate) rather than any single dramatic packet-level anomaly. Relevant to RQ4 (explanation robustness) because a detector that fundamentally depends on aggregating many flows over time suggests single-flow, per-row textual serializations (our Route A input) may only partially capture the true discriminative signal for this class — a useful point for the discussion/limitations section.
- **Stated limitations**: The detector requires aggregating data over comparatively large time windows and across multiple hosts, so it is not designed for real-time, single-connection classification; validated at one large research site and may need re-tuning for networks with different baseline failure-rate statistics; attacks staying below the statistical change-point threshold, or that mimic legitimate failure-rate distributions, could evade detection.
- **Quotable finding (paraphrased)**: Distributed SSH brute-forcing is best understood as a coordinated, long-duration (up to 1.5 months), low-and-slow campaign spread across many source IPs that individually look unremarkable — about 70% of which are simultaneously hitting other independent networks — meaning brute-force traffic is fundamentally a multi-flow, temporal-aggregate phenomenon rather than a single obviously anomalous connection.

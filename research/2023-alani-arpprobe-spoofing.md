- **Dataset(s)**: **Fully confirmed from the primary PDF, Section 4 ("Datasets") — resolves the prior `[VERIFICAR]`.** Two datasets are used:
  1. **Main/training dataset — "IoT-ID"**, the *IoT Network Intrusion Dataset* by Kang, Ahn, Lee, Yoo, Park & Kim (2019) (paper's reference [23]: "H. Kang, D.H. Ahn, G.M. Lee, J.D. Yoo, K.H. Park, H.K. Kim, IoT network intrusion dataset, 2019"). Captured from an IoT environment with two smart-home devices — a voice-controlled virtual assistant (Nugu NU 100, SK Telecom) and a Wi-Fi camera (EZVIZ C2C Mini O Plus 1080P) — plus other connected devices (laptops, smartphones). Contains ARP spoofing, DoS-SYN flooding, scanning attack, and Mirai botnet traffic (UDP flooding, ACK flooding, HTTP flooding, host discovery, Telnet brute-force), stored across 42 raw pcap files. After excluding non-ARP-spoofing attack files (since the paper focuses only on ARP spoofing), the working dataset had **279,105 packets (214,459 benign, 64,646 malicious)**, later class-balanced via random oversampling to 428,918 samples (214,459 each class).
  2. **Second/validation dataset**, used "to ensure that the model generalizes beyond the training data" — from **Hamza, Gharakheili, Benson & Sivaraman (2019), "Detecting Volumetric Attacks on IoT Devices via SDN-Based Monitoring of MUD Activity," ACM SIGCOMM Symposium on SDN Research (SOSR 2019), pp. 36–48** (paper's reference [24]). An SDN-based dataset with multiple IoT attack types including ARP spoofing, TCP SYN flooding, and ping of death. The authors randomly selected 10,000 ARP-spoofing packets and 10,000 benign packets from it for the validation round.
  Both are named, publicly documented datasets — this fully resolves the prior "names of the two evaluation datasets" open item.
- **Models / Methods**: Deep-learning ARP-spoofing detector ("ARP-PROBE") — a multi-layer perceptron DNN with architecture **21 (input) → 24 → 16 → 16 → 8 → 1 (output)**, ReLU hidden activations, sigmoid output (binary classification), Nadam optimizer, binary cross-entropy loss, batch size 1024, 25 epochs, learning rate 0.001, He-normal weight initialization (architecture selected via the TALOS hyperparameter-tuning package). 21 packet-level features were selected (Table 2 of the PDF: frame.len, ip.proto, ip.len, ip.ttl, ip.flags, ip.hdr_len, arp, tcp.flags.syn, tcp.flags.ack, tcp.window_size, icmp, tcp.checksum.status, tcp.dstport, tcp.srcport, tcp.flags, tcp.len, tcp.time_delta, tcp.urgent_pointer, udp.srcport, udp.dstport — no host-specific IP/MAC addresses, to enable generalization). SHAP (DeepExplainer) is applied post-hoc for feature-level explainability.
- **Key metrics reported**: On the held-out 25% test split of the main (IoT-ID) dataset: **accuracy 99.9%, precision 99.9%, recall 99.9%, F1-score 0.999**, false positive rate 0.026%, false negative rate 0.0018% (confirmed via confusion matrix, Fig. 3, and Table 6). 10-fold cross-validation: mean accuracy/precision/recall/F1 = 0.999883/0.999884/0.999884/0.999883, std dev 0.000042 (very low variance, Table 5). On the **second dataset**: accuracy 99.98%, F1-score 0.999, false positive rate 0.020%, false negative rate 0.0009% (confirmed, Table 6 and Section 8 conclusion) — consistent with the first dataset, confirming generalization.
- **XAI method (if any)**: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), DeepExplainer variant, applied to explain the DNN's feature-level decisions. Top feature by SHAP impact: `ip.flags` (the IP header's fragmentation flags field), followed by `frame.len`, `tcp.flags.syn`, `tcp.dstport`, `tcp.flags.ack`, `tcp.srcport`, `tcp.len`, `tcp.checksum.status`, `tcp.flags.reset`, `udp.srcport` (Fig. 6, SHAP summary plot for top 10 features).
- **Relevance to our RQs**: Directly relevant to RQ2 (spoofing feature ground truth) and methodologically relevant to RQ2/RQ4 as a same-domain precedent for applying SHAP to an IoT spoofing detector. Confirms that ARP-spoofing traffic is characterized by abnormal IP-header fragmentation flags, frame length, and TCP SYN/ACK flag patterns rather than host-identifying features (IP/MAC addresses deliberately excluded) — a useful domain-knowledge cross-reference for our own SHAP/Captum analysis of CICIoT2023's ARP-spoofing (Spoofing-ARP) class.
- **Stated limitations**: Confirmed from PDF Section 7 (Discussion): (1) in a high-traffic environment (e.g., smart city scale), the proposed system may become a network bottleneck, because the feature extraction is done at the packet level rather than the network-flow level (deployments in lower-traffic environments such as an industrial IoT or smart home would not be affected); (2) resource-constrained IoT devices might not be capable of delivering acceptable detection performance in addition to their original purpose — mitigated by deploying ARP-PROBE on a dedicated low-cost device analyzing traffic for multiple IoT devices on a local network, rather than running it on-device.
- **Quotable finding (paraphrased)**: A purpose-built, SHAP-explained MLP-DNN detector for ARP spoofing in IoT networks (trained on the Kang et al. 2019 IoT Network Intrusion Dataset and validated on the independent Hamza et al. 2019 SDN/MUD-monitoring dataset) reports near-ceiling accuracy (99.9–99.98%) with very low false-negative rates on both datasets, with SHAP identifying IP-header fragmentation flags and TCP SYN/ACK flag patterns — rather than host-identifying features — as the dominant discriminating signal, consistent with the underlying ARP-level signal being an unusually strong and low-ambiguity feature. A useful benchmark for judging whether a flow-level transformer or tabular baseline trained on CICIoT2023's Ettercap-generated ARP-spoofing traffic converges on comparably specific evidence.

# ExAI5G: A Logic-Based Explainable AI Framework for Intrusion Detection in 5G Networks
- **Authors**: Saeid Sheikhi, Panos Kostakos, Lauri Loven
- **Year / Venue**: 2026, arXiv preprint (submitted 2026-04-20, v1). Peer-review status: still preprint-only as of this pass (re-checked via arXiv journal-ref/comments field — none present — Semantic Scholar `publicationVenue` = "arXiv.org" only, and DBLP; see preprint re-check note below).
- **DOI**: 10.48550/arXiv.2604.18052 (arXiv preprint DOI)
- **URL**: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18052
- **Cluster**: 3
- **Dataset(s)**: **Resolved from the primary PDF (`research/2604.18052v1.pdf`), Sections 4 and the Ethics statement — resolves the prior `[VERIFICAR]`.** The dataset is **NOT a named public benchmark** (confirmed not to be CICIoT2023, 5G-NIDD, or any other externally citable dataset — no dataset citation is given anywhere in the paper, unlike the paper's own literature-review citations for other IDS datasets). It is a **custom, self-collected dataset gathered by the authors on their own fully controlled 5G testbed**, with all attack traffic **synthetically generated** (confirmed verbatim from the Ethics statement, p.24: "The network traffic dataset used in this study was collected in a fully controlled 5G testbed without real user traffic or personally identifiable information. All attack scenarios were synthetically generated for research purposes."). Composition (Table 1): **9 classes (8 attack types + benign)** — Benign, Brute Force, DDoS, Device Spoofing, DoS_MQTT, Eavesdropping, MITM, SQL Injection, Unauthorized Data Access — with **1,753,454 training records** and **194,829 test records**, 29 packet/flow-level features spanning frame-, IP-, TCP-, UDP-, and HTTP-layer properties (Table 2). The paper's own stated limitation (Section 7) confirms this framing: "Dataset Scope: The evaluation is conducted on a single, collected, comprehensive, 5G/IoT dataset. The performance and the extracted rules may not generalize perfectly to different network environments" — i.e., the authors themselves characterize it as their own single collected dataset, not an established public benchmark reused from elsewhere.
- **Models / Methods**: A **Transformer-like network** (not a pretrained language-model encoder): each feature vector is projected into a 128-dimensional embedding, a learnable [CLS] token is appended, and the sequence is processed through a Transformer encoder (**6 layers, 8 heads**) with a linear classifier head over the [CLS] output for the 9 classes. Trained with a focal-loss objective (class-weighted) and AdamW, tuned learning rate 1e-4 and weight decay 1e-2 (grid search), early stopping on validation macro-F1. This is followed by: (1) **Integrated Gradients (IG)** feature attribution (100 sampled test instances, zero-vector baseline, attributions aggregated by mean absolute value); (2) a **CART decision-tree surrogate** (max depth=4, min leaf size=40) trained on the Transformer's own predictions as pseudo-labels, from which logical if-then rules are extracted per leaf; (3) an **LLM-mediated natural-language explanation module** using four generator LLMs (Qwen2.5:14b, Llama3.1:8b, Phi4:14b, Gemma3:27b) plus a separate, more powerful evaluator LLM (Llama3.3:70b) that scores explanation actionability on a 1–5 scale.
- **Key metrics reported**: Confirmed from the PDF (Table 3, Table 4, Section 5): overall **accuracy 99.87%**, **macro-F1 = 0.854** (weighted-avg F1 = 0.999, reflecting severe class imbalance — Benign support 148,070 vs. e.g. Device Spoofing support only 8). Baseline comparison (Table 4, macro-F1 on validation set): Decision Tree 0.487, MLP 0.887, XGBoost 0.966, CatBoost 0.970, LightGBM 0.980, **Random Forest 0.989** (best) vs. the proposed Transformer's 0.854 — i.e., several opaque baselines actually *outperform* the Transformer on macro-F1, a trade-off the authors explicitly frame as "a necessary trade-off for achieving full interpretability" (Discussion, Section 6). Surrogate decision-tree: **16 extracted logical rules, 99.72% ± 0.01% fidelity, 100% coverage**; after pruning the least-supported leaf, 15 rules retain high fidelity while covering 99.998% of test cases. Feature attribution: `frame.time_relative`, `tcp.time_relative`, and `tcp.stream` are the top three most influential features by mean |IG| (Fig. 5) — temporal/volumetric features dominate. LLM explanation quality (Table 5, averaged over 20 test instances): Qwen2.5:14b and Phi4:14b achieve the highest attribution faithfulness (0.99 and 0.98) and actionability scores (4.6 and 4.5 on a 1–5 scale); Gemma3:27b lower faithfulness (0.91). A small two-expert human study (Table 7) rated explanations more conservatively (faithfulness 3.3–4.1, actionability 3.3–4.1 on 1–5 scale) than the automatic LLM-as-judge metrics, showing the automatic scores are somewhat optimistic. Computational efficiency: median inference latency 2.48 ms per flow on CPU.
- **XAI method (if any)**: Integrated Gradients for feature attribution, combined with surrogate decision-tree rule extraction and LLM-generated (and LLM-evaluated) narrative explanations — a three-stage explainability pipeline, with a novel quantitative validation scheme (semantic similarity, attribution faithfulness, actionability score) plus a small human expert-rating study.
- **Relevance to our RQs**: Relevant to **RQ2** as a worked example of Integrated-Gradients-based feature attribution on a transformer classifier over tabular-derived features (via a small custom Transformer encoder rather than a textualized-flow pretrained encoder like ours), and offers a template for extending our discussion section toward rule-extraction/narrative explanation as future work. Also a useful cautionary data point for RQ1: this paper explicitly shows a case where an opaque baseline (Random Forest, macro-F1 0.989) outperforms an explainable Transformer (macro-F1 0.854) on the same task — directly parallels our own accuracy/explainability trade-off framing (our DistilBERT 0.707 macro-F1 vs. XGBoost 0.734). Most relevant to **RQ4**: the authors explicitly identify explanation-robustness-under-adversarial-perturbation as unaddressed ("Adversarial Robustness... has not yet been evaluated"), directly corroborating the gap our paper targets.
- **Stated limitations**: Confirmed from PDF Section 7 ("Limitations and Future Work"): (1) **Dataset Scope** — single, self-collected 5G/IoT dataset; generalization to other network environments/vendors not tested (explicit future work: cross-dataset validation on datasets "from different 5G core network vendors and IoT deployment scenarios"); (2) **Surrogate Model Fidelity** — the CART surrogate achieves high but imperfect fidelity (99.72%), so "a small fraction of the deep model's decisions are not captured by the logic rules," leaving a remaining area of opacity; (3) **Adversarial Robustness** — explicitly unaddressed: "An adversary with knowledge of the extracted rules could potentially craft malicious traffic specifically designed to evade detection. The adversarial robustness of this explanation-driven framework has not yet been evaluated," flagged as critical future work.
- **Quotable finding (paraphrased)**: A small Transformer classifier (6 layers, 8 heads, 128-dim) reaches 99.87% accuracy (but only 0.854 macro-F1, below several opaque baselines such as Random Forest at 0.989) on a self-collected, synthetically generated 5G/IoT intrusion dataset, and its Integrated-Gradients-derived explanations can be distilled into a small set of high-fidelity logical rules (99.72% fidelity, 16 rules) plus LLM-narrated natural-language explanations validated as faithful and actionable by both an LLM judge and human security experts — but the authors explicitly acknowledge they have not tested whether these explanations remain stable under adversarial or naturally occurring input perturbations, leaving explanation robustness as open future work — the same gap our RQ4 is designed to close.
